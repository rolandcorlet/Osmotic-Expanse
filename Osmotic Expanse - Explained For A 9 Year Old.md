# The Osmotic Expanse — Explained For A 9-Year-Old

## First: what kind of game is it?

It is a **make-believe game you play with friends around a table**, the same family as Dungeons & Dragons. Nobody runs around. You talk. You describe what your character does ("I swim up to the rusty gate and jam my arm into it"), you roll some **six-sided dice**, and the dice tell you whether it worked. You also draw on paper and keep a character sheet with numbers on it.

One friend is the **Director**. The Director is like a mix between a teacher, a referee, and the person who built the world. They tell you what you see and smell, they play the monsters and the shopkeepers, and they decide how hard a job is. Everyone else plays one living creature.

There are four books: the **Core Rulebook** (all the rules), the **Player's Atlas** (how to make your character), the **Director's Codex** (how to run the world), and the **Lytic Bestiary** (sixty-two monsters). But you only need a handful of rules to start.

## The world: you are tiny, and everything is inside a living thing

Imagine the inside of a living body, but enormous — as big as a universe. There is **no sun, no ground, no sky**. Everything floats in warm fluid, like very thin soup. Instead of oceans there are **Great Flows**: rivers of food that carry you if you let them. Instead of mountains there are **protein ranges** and giant **protein bridges**. Instead of cities there are colonies and factories the size of weather. Instead of a night sky it is simply dark, and the light you see is other people glowing.

You are not a human. You are an **organism** — a tiny living thing shaped like something from inside a body. Your skin is called a **membrane**, and it is the most important thing about you, because a membrane decides what gets in and what stays out. That's where the name *Osmotic Expanse* comes from: osmosis is how water pushes through a skin, and in this world almost everything — health, magic, talking, travelling, fighting — is really about water, pressure, and skins.

The story behind it all: a very long time ago, one drop of something called the **Prime Serum** fell into a plain, mindless puddle, and the puddle learned to *keep going*. Four million days later, its descendants built civilisations. Some of them believe the drop was on purpose. That argument is one of the big adventures.

Two ideas matter most, and they are the personality of the whole game:

- **Nothing is wasted.** If you beat a monster, you can harvest it. Harvest enough and you can *attach its parts to yourself* and get its abilities. In this game, treasure is not gold — it's evolution you haven't used yet.
- **Everything leaks.** You are a bag of water, and the whole game is about staying that way.

## Who can you be?

You pick a **Lineage** — your body plan and your people. Six of them:

| Lineage | Body | Best at | Weakness |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Amoeban** | no fixed shape | chemistry, crafting, sneaking | hard to wear armour |
| **Epithalian** | flat sheets and layers | building, toughness | slow |
| **Flagellan** | whips and fins like a tail | speed, swimming, dodging | fragile |
| **Lysosan** | quiet, doubled, sleepy | senses, patience | people distrust them |
| **Immunan** | spiky, recognising | leading, commanding, protecting | rigid, literal |
| **Barophil** | dense, heavy, armoured | brute strength, not moving | very slow to react |

Then you pick a **Phenotype** — that's the class, like "healer" or "sniper" in other games. There are 24, with names like *Current Rider* (a courier who rides rivers), *Membrane Breacher* (a door-breaker), *Enzymatic Splicer* (a chemist), *Acidic Corrosor* (a dissolve-y monster-slayer). Each class gives you special tricks and lets you get stronger in four big steps called **Stages**.

## The one rule that runs everything

Almost every uncertain thing in the game uses the same single rule. Learn it once and you know the whole game.

**Step 1 — build a pool of dice.** You take your natural talent in something (a number from 1 to 6) and add how much you practised it (also up to 4), plus bonuses for tools or friends. That total is how many d6 you roll. So 5 dice means you roll five six-sided dice and add them up.

**Step 2 — 1s are nasty.** Any die that shows a **1** counts as **zero**, and it also marks **1 Stress** on your Stress track. This is the clever part of the game: the more dice you roll, the more chances you have of poisoning your own good luck. Rolling lots of dice helps a lot, but never perfectly.

**Step 3 — beat the number.** Every job has an **Activation Threshold**:

| Number | How the game describes it |
| --: | :-- |
| 8 | easy — anyone trained can do this |
| 12 | normal — a good specialist usually manages |
| 16 | hard — needs real skill or a good plan |
| 20 | very hard — takes a whole colony |
| 24 | almost impossible |
| 28 | once-in-a-generation |

**Step 4 — leftovers are never wasted.** If you needed 12 and rolled 17, you didn't just succeed — you succeeded by 5. Those 5 points are called **Overflow**, and they always buy something extra: more damage, more distance, better crafting, a secret someone lets slip, an extra hole in your enemy. So you never roll twice for one attempt.

**Step 5 — the super-success.** If your roll has **no 1s at all and at least two 6s**, that's **Runaway Catalysis**: the reaction feeds itself, and your effect happens twice as strong plus a bonus. It happens maybe one time in ten, which is why it feels amazing.

There's one more piece of fairness that's worth knowing: the Director is **not allowed to secretly make the number harder** because your group got stronger. Instead they make the *story* harder — a bigger hole, a rougher current, a better tool. Numbers don't move; the world does.

And sometimes you **don't roll at all**. If nobody's fighting you and there's no hurry, you just succeed — the game assumes you could try until you got it. You only roll when failing would actually cost you something.

## Your five numbers and your practice

You have five natural talents, and they're honestly pretty normal ideas:

- **Viscosity (VSC)** — how thick and solid you are. Strength and toughness.
- **Diffusion (DFF)** — how fast and nimble you are. Speed and balance.
- **Reactivity (RTC)** — how clever you are with chemicals. This is the "magic" and crafting talent.
- **Osmosis (OSM)** — how well you notice things. Smell, instinct, not being fooled.
- **Affinity (AFF)** — how well you talk, lead, and make friends.

Against those you have **Pathways** — things you've practised, like *Swimming*, *Hiding*, *Piercing armour*, *Crafting*, *Sense-smelling*, *Talking*. Practice ranks go from "never learned" to "there's a statue of you". So a roll is: talent + practice + help, in dice.

## How fighting works

Fighting is called a **Pulse War**, and it's less "you hit me, I hit you" and more "everyone is thrashing around in a current, fast."

The world isn't drawn on a grid of squares. Distance is measured in **Drifts** (about two metres), and enemies are grouped into bands: **Contact** (touching), **Near**, **Mid**, **Far**, **Loose** (out of range). Weapons say which band they can reach.

Each round (a **Cycle**, about six seconds) you get a bucket of **Pulse Points** — usually 3 at the start of the game, up to 8 when you're a veteran. Everything costs points out of that bucket:

- swim somewhere: 1 point
- catch your breath / calm down: 1 point
- look around for ambushes: 1 point
- attack: 2 points (you can attack twice if you have the points)
- do quick crafting, throw a potion, patch a friend: 2 points
- use a big power: 3 to 5 points
- **hold 1 back**: you keep it to react *during somebody else's turn* — block a hit, dodge a shove, or mess up their roll

That held point is the heartbeat of the combat. You aren't just waiting to be hit; you get one interrupt per round.

To hit something you roll your attack pool against the target's **Membrane Threshold** (usually around 15 — that's how hard it is to land a blow on someone quick). If you beat it, you deal damage equal to your **Overflow + your weapon's damage + your power's bonus − their armour**. Then, depending on the *kind* of damage, something extra happens: a big shove pushes them into another band, acid makes their armour brittle, electricity stops them using their powers, a virus steals one of their tricks for two rounds.

And one fun rule: if you swim **three Drifts in a straight line** and then attack in the same round, you get **+2 dice**. Speed becomes strength.

## What happens when you get hurt

Your health is called **Membrane Integrity**, or MI — literally "how much of you is still inside you."

When MI hits zero, **you do not die.** You go **Dormant**: knocked out, leaking, helpless. Anyone standing next to you can spend a round patching you up and bring you back. That's important, so the game says it out loud: *your friends are never helpless as long as one of them can still move.*

But while you're Dormant, a clock ticks. You fill in **Lysis Countdown** boxes (usually five to seven of them), and you should write them on your sheet in ink so you can see how close you are. If the boxes fill up, that's **Total Lysis** — real death, your body becomes part of the world and can be harvested.

Even then the game gives you an out: your friends can rebuild you, but it's expensive and difficult, and you come back with a **permanent scar** — a real rule that stays on your sheet, like "one skill permanently worse", "you leak until someone fixes you", "when something hits you very hard you splash acid on it", or "a monster species now thinks you're family, and will help you once and then come to collect." Dying in this game doesn't reset you. It *changes* you.

There's also **Stress** — the track that those ugly 1s fill up. Stress isn't injury; it's your chemistry getting messy. At 3 Stress you lose a die from everything, at 5 you lose two, at 8 you collapse. You clear it by resting, or by spending a round and a little energy just to breathe, which the game calls **Vent**. A lot of the tension in this game isn't "will I get hit" — it's "how many bad dice can I afford before I stop being able to aim."

## Your three coins (and why loot is evolution)

The game has three resources, and they're very tidy:

- **ATP** — energy. You spend it on powers, sprinting, and forcing things. It's also money: in this world you literally *digest* the toll you pay. You can only carry as much as your own body holds.
- **Substrate (S)** — raw stuff. Food, goo, minerals. You use it to buy gear, to craft, and to patch holes in yourself. You can only carry so much, so you have to choose what to bring.
- **Codon** — this is experience, and it's never spent. It just piles up, and when the pile gets big enough you **Grow** a level. Killing monsters gives Codon, but so does *surviving something*: rescuing a friend, mapping a hazard, saving a colony.

Then there's the fourth thing, and it's the best idea in the game: **Specimens**. Every monster lists what you can harvest off it — ordinary tissue, rare organs, perfect genetic sequences. Those are the ingredients for crafting gear, learning powers, and **grafting** monster parts onto your own body. The monster that nearly killed you becomes the armour piece that saves you from its cousin. Every entry in the monster book is designed backwards from that sentence.

## "Magic" is just chemistry you do on purpose

There's no magic, there are **Bursts** — quick, powerful reactions you trigger by paying energy and action points. They come in six flavours:

- **Protean** — fold and build: nets, shields, bridges, cages, walls
- **Lytic** — dissolve: acid spit, corrosive fog, eat armour
- **Osmotic** — pressure: shove, crush, drag, knock someone into the next band
- **Thermic** — hot and cold: boiling, freezing, cauterising
- **Galvanic** — electricity and signals: stun, mark, interrupt, hijack timing
- **Somatic** — flesh: heal, grow, mutate, reshape

Armour comes in five families, and it's rock-paper-scissors: a fatty lipid weave stops water-pressure but is terrible against acid; a hard shell stops acid but is terrible against pressure and slows you down; a goo mantle stops heat and electricity but not viruses; living graft armour heals itself — but you have to feed it energy every round or it stops working. Choosing armour isn't picking the biggest number, it's deciding **what you're willing to be wrong about**.

## The monsters, in seven families

The Bestiary sorts its 62 creatures into **Orders**, and each one plays completely differently:

1. **Lytic Swarms** — clouds of tiny dissolvers. They attack the biggest thing they can see and eat the floor. They have no flanks.
2. **Protein Sentinels** — armoured guards. Barely move, huge armour, and — here's the twist — *they'll negotiate* if you aren't threatening whatever they guard.
3. **Nutrient Drifters** — huge slow whale-things. Mostly harmless, extremely edible, and the reason whole colonies have arguments about who's allowed to hunt them.
4. **Pathogenic Wraiths** — information looking for a body. They don't want to fight you; they want to *wear* you, steal your special moves, and come back later wearing your face.
5. **Osmotic Brutes** — walking pressure. They don't try to hurt you so much as change where you are. Their favourite answer to a fight is "wager" or "formal challenge".
6. **Immune Sentinels** — the police. They *recognise* you, arrest you, and get better the longer the fight lasts because they're learning your smell. If you show valid paperwork, some of them stop fighting.
7. **Anomalies** — places where the world's rules stopped working. The only monsters you cannot learn: the trick that worked last time will kill you this time.

Plus hazards: acid currents, dry zones, boiling vents, gel fields that cost double to swim through. The terrain is an active player in every fight.

## The world keeps moving

Time is biological. A day is a **Metabolic Beat**. Rest happens in **Fugue Intervals**. The seasons are **Osmotic Tides**, and they're about salt and water: an *Isotonic* season is calm, a *Hypertonic* season sucks water out of everything and is a crisis, an *Acidic Surge* season is a disaster.

There are six factions to make friends or enemies with: a giant industrial factory-government, the registration police, the river-trade guilds, the recyclers, a black market for stolen DNA, and a deep religion that hears the world's original drop. Your group also has a shared track called **Cohesion** that grows when you keep promises, rescue each other and eat together — and you can spend it to hand a friend an extra die, refuse a Stress mark, or wake a bonded animal companion. If it hits zero, the group gets worse at everything until someone repairs the friendship. Talking is its own mechanic: you argue with *chemicals*, not just words, and your character carries an **Oath** — a promise written into your body, with little boxes that fill up when you break it.

## What an actual turn feels like

Say you play **Siss**, a Flagellan courier: super fast, super fragile.

*"I want to ride the current at the guard and knife his arm, not his body."* The Director says: that's a **Strike** — 2 points. You roll your speed (5) plus your weapon practice (0, you never learned it) = **5 dice**. You roll 6, 6, 4, 3, 1. The 1 counts zero and marks Stress. Total 19 against the guard's Membrane Threshold of 15. Hit, with 4 Overflow — but look at the dice: two 6s and… no, there's a 1, so no super-success. Damage lands, plus the 4 Overflow, and because you swam three Drifts in a straight line you had +2 dice. The guard gets shoved into the Near band.

Now the guard swings back, and you spend the point you held: his target number against you goes up by 4, and he misses. Next round you have 3 less luck in you because of that Stress — and that's the story.

## Getting stronger

When your **Codon** pile gets big enough, your character **Grows** — that's levelling up, and at certain levels you hit a whole new **Stage**, which is almost like becoming a slightly different animal: more health, more energy, a new trick, and a free bump to one talent or one skill. Between adventures you also **craft**: you spend Substrate and monster Specimens to build a new weapon, mix potions (the game calls them **Vials**), or have surgery to attach a graft. There's a simple hundred-sided **Harvest table** the Director rolls on after a big win, like a loot table, except everything on it is a body part, a chemical, or a strange living tool.

Resting comes in two sizes. A **Reconstitution** is a short rest — about an hour of floating and settling down, which refills your energy and clears Stress. A **Dormant Cycle** is a long rest — six hours curled up in a safe nest, which refills your health and erases those scary Countdown boxes. The Director is told never to give you two full long rests in a row, because feeling a little unsafe is what makes the world feel real.

## The five things to remember

1. **Roll the right number of dice, beat the number, 1s are bad, extra is bonus.** That is basically the entire game.
2. **You're a bag of water with opinions**, and every rule is about staying closed while letting useful things in.
3. **Loot is a body part.** Kill it, harvest it, become a bit of it.
4. **Knocked down isn't dead.** You go Dormant, a friend patches you, and if you do die, you come back changed instead of reset.
5. **The rules don't cheat.** Numbers stay honest; the world gets harder instead.

The whole game wants one thing from you: *decide what you're willing to let inside you, and pay for it.*
