# WHAT THE WATER OWED
## A Tale of Sump Nine, as told at the Rendering
### *The Osmotic Expanse* — a story of one Osmotic Tide

> *Every body is a debt. Some are paid in Substrate, some in labour, some in Oath. The ones we sing about were paid in water.*
> — a proverb of the Pump

---

## THE HEARTH

Sit closer. No — closer than that; the old ones at the back need to smell the beginning, and a beginning is the faintest part of any trail.

You know where you are. This is the Rendering, the second Beat of it, the Isotonic Fall, and the dead of Sump Nine are hanging in the Ash Rows behind you, waiting their turn to be read. You have eaten. Your Stress is gone; the meal took it, as the meal is meant to. The Reader is busy and the Wardens are drunk on salt-tea and the haulers' children are asleep in a pile by the warm wall, and there is nothing left to do this dim but listen.

So I will give you a trail.

My name is Lisk-of-the-Seven-Ladders. I am a Pheromological Messenger, which is a grand way of saying that I carry other people's words across the water for money, and sometimes, when the money is bad and the Hearth is good, I carry one of my own. You can taste my lineage — the fins, the wake, the salt of the Great Flows on everything I say. Flagellan. A drifter. I have not stood still for more than a Fugue Interval since I was budded, and I will be pacing behind you before this is done. Forgive me. It is in the body.

This trail belongs to Sump Nine. Some of you were alive for it. Some of you were children in the pile by that wall. One of you — I can smell you, Registrar, don't hide behind the Muster — one of you is *in* it. When I get to the part that is yours, you may stop me. You won't. You never could stop a true thing once it started.

It is the story of a hauler named Oro, and the Tide that took everything from him except the one thing nobody can take from a body: what it is owed.

Breathe in. Here is the first scent.

---

## I · THE SEVENTH LADDER
### *Isotonic Rise*

> *A colony is a body. Find the organ that is hurting, and you will find the story.*
> — the haulers' Hearth-saying

The Pump woke Oro the way it woke every hauler in Sump Nine: not with sound, because nobody in the Expanse has ears worth the name, but with *pressure*. At the first Fugue Interval of every lit Beat the great somatic gate at the channel mouth flexes, and the whole sump shudders with the water it lets in, and every body in every hab feels the colony draw breath.

Oro was already moving when it came. He was always already moving. He had slept — if you can call it sleep — wedged in the hauler-racks of Hab Four with his lashes trailing in the outflow, because a Flagellan who lies still too long wakes up with nothing in him: no strength, no Pulse, nothing but a dull grey need to be going somewhere. The old haulers called it *the ache*. Oro had never in his life gone long enough without moving to find out what came after the ache. He suspected it was death, and he suspected it was boring.

He was nineteen Drift Seasons old that Rise. Young for a hauler, old for a ward. He was long and pale-finned and had the narrow, hungry body of the **Swollen** — the water-heavy poor who lived in the low habs by the outflows, where everything was cheap because everything was wet. You could see his condition from across the sump. Everyone could. That was the point of conditions.

The morning Rite at the haulers' Hearth was short, because haulers' Rites always were. Old Sefa led it, as she had led it for longer than Oro had been alive: a slow turn around the warm stone, every body brushing every other body, a shared breath of the Hearth's scent so that the day would begin with everyone smelling, a little, of each other. *We are one organ*, said the scent. *We carry what the colony cannot carry itself.*

Sefa was Oro's root-elder. She was the oldest living organism of the Seven Ladders budline — the hauling family whose founder, six Great Cycles ago, had cut seven ladders up the sump's north wall so that water could be carried to the habs above without waiting for the gate. There were still seven ladders. There were not many Seven Ladders left: Sefa, two cousins in the Wastes, a quarrelsome aunt in Knot Nine, and Oro. And Oro, everyone agreed, did not smell much like a Seven Ladders, though nobody ever said so to Sefa's face.

"You're thinking," she said as the Rite broke up. Her lashes were grey and slow now; she moved like water in a cold pipe. "I can smell it. It smells like rust."

"I'm counting," Oro said.

"Counting what?"

"What we owe."

She made the sound that, in an old Flagellan, passes for laughter: a puff of sour bubbles. "You'll never finish. Go and haul."

---

Here is a Beat in Sump Nine. I want you to have it whole, because nobody ever writes the ordinary down, and then the ordinary is gone and nobody remembers what all the songs were *for*.

Twenty Fugue Intervals of light and four of dim; that is a Beat. In the first two the Hearths wake, the Rites are breathed, and the Membrane opens its registers — the Porter, a stiff Immunan called Ket, stands at the somatic gate and reads every organism in or out, and the gate itself, being alive, reads them too. From the third to the tenth is the first shift. That is when the Pump works.

The Pump is not a machine. It is *us*. It is a hundred and forty haulers on the seven ladders and the four tow-lines, carrying sealed cysts of fresh water from the channel mouth up to the habs on the high wall, where the Settled live, and higher still to the sealed pockets where the Dry live — the dense, salted, comfortable few who keep their interiors concentrated and their doors shut. Water flows down on its own. Everyone knows that. Getting it to go *up* costs a body.

Oro hauled four cysts up the third ladder that morning before the shudder of the gate had faded from his skin. Each cyst was half his own weight. Each rung was a little argument between his lashes and the drift. Haulers learn early that you do not carry water uphill with your body; you carry it with your *metabolism*, ATP burning like a lamp in your chest, and when the lamp runs low you start paying in something worse. By the fifth cyst his Stress was rising, the faint fizzing wrongness in the membranes that every organism learns to read in itself, and he stopped on the fourth landing to Vent — a hard, ugly exhalation of spent chemistry that cleared his head and cost him energy he'd need later.

"Don't," said the hauler above him on the ladder, a scarred Barophil called Dunmore who had been hauling since before the last Rereading and moved like geology. "Vent at the top. Not halfway. Halfway, your body learns that halfway is where you stop."

"My body learns everything," Oro said. "That's the problem."

Four S a Beat, a hauler earns. Six if he carries uphill all shift. A colonist's whole Beat of work is worth five S to the colony; that is what the Tally-Keeper writes in the book, and every price in Sump Nine is read against it. Four S feeds a Swollen hauler, if he eats at the Hearth and sleeps in the racks and buys nothing. It does not pay a debt.

And the Seven Ladders had a debt. Every budline has a debt. Ours — I mean theirs; forgive me, the trail gets into my mouth — was a hundred S borrowed from the Hegemony's Brine Office two Great Cycles ago, at five S a Beat, to rebuild the ladders after a collapse. The principal had never been paid. The interest had been paid, Beat after Beat, by Seven Ladders after Seven Ladders, for longer than anyone alive could remember. *A budline's debt divides with it*, the saying goes, *and never gets smaller.* When Oro had come of age four Drift Seasons before — when Sefa had stood him at the Hearth and spoken his root name aloud for the first time, *Oro-of-the-Seven-Ladders* — he had inherited his share. It was written in his Ledger now: one line, in the column that says *You owe*.

*Oro owes the Brine Office his share of the Ladders' debt: five S a Beat until the principal is paid. Witnessed by the Registrar.*

He had read that line so many times he could smell it in his sleep.

---

The eleventh and twelfth Intervals are the shared meal, and in Sump Nine the charter says that every hab feeds one nursery shift, so that day Oro ate fast and then went to the nursery Hearth to feed the young.

I want to tell you about the nursery, because it matters later, and because if you have never fed a nursery you have not lived in a colony.

Sump Nine's nursery sat at the bottom of the gel moat, where the water was warmest and the currents gentlest — forty-odd buds of every lineage, none older than a Drift Season or two, all of them dependents, all of them rolling through the world a little clumsier than they would one day be. They could not swear Oaths. They could not be listed yet. They existed in the colony's Ledger only as lines on their parents' pages: *owed care*. They smelled of milk-chemistry and fear and delight, and they were the only organisms in Sump Nine who did not know what condition they were.

Oro liked them. Buds don't ask what you are. They ask *what are you doing*, which is a much better question.

He was teaching a squall of Amoeban buds to roll a food-cyst between them without dissolving it — Amoebans are born wanting to eat everything, and the trick is to teach them that some things are for *passing* — when the nursery's keeper, a broad-sheeted Epithalian called Tannet, drifted over and said quietly, "The protein supports are cut."

"What?"

"The lattice under the north wall of the moat. Somebody's cut the supports. For salvage, I'd guess. Protein lattice sells." Tannet's sheets were tight with anger. "It'll hold for now. If the gate flexes hard it won't."

Oro looked at the lattice. Somebody *had* cut it — neat, deliberate, the way a Vat Crew would cut a carcass. You could still smell the tool.

"Tell the Steward," he said.

"There *is* no Steward," Tannet said. "There hasn't been since the last one went Dormant at the Rendering. Two claimants, and the Assembly can't find eight voices for either. The office is empty and so is the store." The Epithalian's sheets rippled. "Tell the Muster, then. Tell the Tally-Keeper. Tell anyone who'll smell it. I'm not leaving forty buds under a wall nobody's holding up."

That was Sump Nine, that Rise. An organ was hurting. Nobody was in charge of it. That is how most stories begin, in the Expanse, and how most colonies end.

---

The second shift runs from the thirteenth Interval to the twentieth, and at the end of it, in every colony large enough to have one, the **Quorum Speaker** reads the air.

You young ones from the Wastes may not have seen a Quorum. Let me tell you, because it is the most beautiful and the most unfair thing in the Expanse.

A colony does not vote. A colony *breathes*. When there is a question before it — *open the gate to the refugees? sell the water rights? let the Hegemony build?* — every organism who cares breathes its signal into the air of the Hearth. Signal is a molecule; it accumulates. And when the air is thick enough with one answer, the colony *knows* — all at once, every body, in the chemistry under its skin. The Quorum Speaker only says it aloud.

That Rise, the question on Sump Nine's air was the question of the empty office: *who will be Steward?* And the air was thick with one name.

*Corvane.*

Corvane of the Sealed Pocket was Epithalian and Dry — so Dry you could taste the salt of him from the bottom of the ladders. He lived in the highest pocket on the wall, behind a pump he did not work, and he had wanted the Steward's office for six Tides. That Rise he was *breathing heavily*: paying for rendered signal, cyst after cyst of it, poured into the Hearth air on his behalf by servants who smelled of nothing at all. That is lawful. That is what it means to be Dry. An organism with more ATP can breathe more signal. The Swollen can breathe as hard as they like; the Dry can buy the air.

The Speaker that season was an old Lysosan called Wend — Honesty four, the gossip went, which is to say honest enough most of the time and not quite honest enough when it mattered. Wend stood at the Hearth at the twentieth Interval and tasted the air and said, carefully, "Corvane gains. Not yet Quorum. The air will be read again at the Contract Fair."

The haulers' Hearth was silent. Every hauler knew what Corvane wanted the Steward's office *for*. A Steward feeds the colony from its store. A Steward may pay a poor household's arrears, or not. A Steward holds the keys to the Vacuole, and the Vacuole holds the water.

"He'll sell it," Dunmore said, in his slow Barophil way. "He'll sell the water rights for the Tide and pay himself a percentage and call it prudence."

"He can't," said one of the young ones. "The charter says the tally isn't negotiable."

"The charter," said Dunmore, "is three sentences. Corvane has a pocket full of lawyers."

---

At the twenty-first Interval the light dims, and Sump Nine closes its Membrane for the night, and the colony goes to its Dormant Cycles. Curfew. The Porter seals the somatic gate and the gate sleeps too, in its way.

But a Flagellan does not sleep in the dim. A Flagellan works the night pumps, because a Flagellan *has* to move, and the colony is glad of it, and pays it two S less for the privilege of its sleeplessness.

So it was Oro who was on the channel tow-line at the twenty-second Interval of the first Beat of the Rise, alone, in the dark, when the wreck came down the Fork of the Second Flow and caught in the grille of the sixth hab's channel.

He smelled it before he saw it. Everyone in the Expanse smells the dead before they see them — that particular sweet slackening of a body whose chemistry has stopped arguing with the water. But this one smelled *wrong*. It smelled of the Wastes, of ash and render-pits and the bitter salt of the far side of the Mid-Strata. It smelled of a long, long drift.

And under all of it, faint as a lie, it smelled of *him*.

He told me later — he told me everything, later — that his first thought was that the water had made a mistake. That the current had picked up some ghost of his scent from the tow-line and laid it on the corpse. You could think that, if you were nineteen and frightened and alone in the dim. But Oro was a hauler. He had carried enough cysts for enough organisms to know the difference between a scent on the outside of a thing and a scent that comes from *inside*.

He hauled her out of the grille. It was a her — a Lysosan, long and fine-boned, with the doubled, quiet look of the latent lineage even in death. A **Pathogen Scout**, by the kit on her: the sealed sample-pouches, the scent-masking film, the travelling-graft along her spine that lets a scout pass through a region's antigens without being read. Three Beats dead, maybe four. Dead of something that had opened her membranes from the inside. Lysosans carry sequences that are not theirs; sometimes one of them wins the argument.

She had not been harvested. Nobody had read her. She had come a long way to be found, and she had been found by the only organism in Sump Nine who smelled like she did.

Wound into the coils of her spine — not hidden, exactly, but tucked, the way you would tuck a letter into a coat you meant someone to find — was a **memory strand**: a sealed crystal of her own recorded moments, the kind that old budlines keep and play back at the Hearth on feast nights. It was warm. Strands stay warm for as long as the memory inside them still wants to be remembered.

And on the seal, in trail-script fine as a hair, someone had laid a name.

*For the one I carried. For the Seven Ladders' ward.*

Oro sat in the dim on the tow-line with a dead stranger across his lashes and read that line over and over, the way he read the line in his Ledger. He did not know yet that it was the first line of a second Ledger — the one the world keeps on you whether you want it to or not.

He knew only this: he had never once, in nineteen Drift Seasons, been called a *ward*.

He did not tell anyone. Not that night. He laid the scout on the channel shelf and wrapped her in tow-line so the current would not take her, and he put the strand in the pouch under his fin where haulers carry their Substrate, and he went back to work, because the night pumps do not care what you have found.

That is the first hook, friends. Hold it. It goes all the way down.

---

## II · THE CONTRACT FAIR
### *Isotonic Rise — the third Beat*

> *Swear it at the Fair, and it costs you nothing to swear. Keep it, and it costs you everything else.*
> — Contract Fair saying

The Contract Fair comes once a Tide, at the Isotonic Rise, when the water is soft and travel is cheap and everyone can afford, for three Beats, to be generous. It is the season of promises. Debts are signed; Oaths are sworn; buds are named; offices are chosen for the Tide to come; and couples who want to conjugate — who want to link their membranes and exchange their sequence and bind their budlines together for good — stand at the Hearth in front of everyone and do it.

Sump Nine's Fair filled the main sump with stalls strung on cables, every one of them breathing a different smell: rendered Substrate from the Wastes, lipid sheet from the Plateaus, cheap licensed grafts from a Hegemony factor's barge, plasmid-sellers from the Tallow Market with their knacks in little sealed vials. Amoeban shape-actors performed on the gel moat — becoming a Warden, becoming a hauler, becoming a Dry merchant so precisely bloated with salt that the haulers' children laughed until they hiccupped bubbles. A Flow Guild courier-mother was taking bets on the current-race at the Swell, two Tides off. There were masks for sale, though masks were not lawful until the Swell, and everybody bought them anyway.

Oro hauled through the first Beat of it with the strand still under his fin and the scout's body still wrapped in tow-line on the channel shelf, and on the second Beat, because he could not bear it any longer, he went to the Registrar.

You will want to know about the Registrar, because she is the strangest organism in this tale, and the kindest.

Her name was Quell. She was Lysosan — the latent lineage, the library-bodies, the ones every ward and gate in the Expanse can see are *not quite standard*. Lysosans are never trusted with Warden offices; the wards will not have them. But Sump Nine had had a purge, three Tides before, when a Warden circuit came through and struck from the register every organism it could not read cleanly. Quell had been one of the struck. She had been **Unlisted** — a body with no legal existence, outside the colony's chemistry, owed nothing, owing nothing, unable to sign or swear or be protected. And the morning after the purge, she had gone back to the Registrar's shelves anyway, and opened the register, and gone on keeping it.

Nobody had stopped her. Nobody could find eight voices to stop her. The Membrane went on being read, the lines went on being written, and the hand that wrote them belonged to someone the lines said did not exist. That was the kind of colony Sump Nine was.

Quell's apprentice was at the shelves when Oro came in. That is how this part of the story starts. That is how the best part of it starts.

The apprentice was Immunan. You could see it from the door: the marking-glands, the clean ranked geometry of the body, the faint cold smell of a lineage that recognises and remembers and is never, ever wrong about what belongs. Young — younger than Oro. Their name was Mesk-of-the-Clean-Count, and they were copying a register line into crystal with a stillness so complete that Oro, who could not be still for one Interval, found it almost obscene.

"I need the Registrar," Oro said.

"She's at the Fair," Mesk said, without looking up. "Choosing the Muster. What do you need?"

And here is the thing you must understand about Immunans, if you are to understand the rest of this. Immunans are *Literal*. It is their Frailty, and it is written in their bodies. They cannot be deceived by language — a lie, to an Immunan, smells like what it is — but they cannot *use* it, either. They cannot deceive. They cannot flatter. And if you ask an Immunan a question, it will answer you truthfully, unless an Oath forbids it, and even then it hurts: every sentence of refusal costs them a little Stress, a little fraying, like a membrane pulled the wrong way.

Oro did not know that, that morning. He was a hauler. He had never talked to an Immunan who wasn't reading him at a gate.

"I found a body," he said, "and it had my name on it."

Mesk looked up.

Mesk told me, much later, that the first thing they noticed about Oro was that he was lying — not about the body, but about being calm. He was moving, very slightly, all the time: lashes, fins, a flicker of the wake. Flagellans always are. But this was more than that. This was a body that had not stopped moving since the dim, because it did not know what would happen if it did.

"Show me," Mesk said.

---

There was a Convocation over the wreck, of course. There is always a Convocation over a wreck.

I have told the story of that Convocation at other Hearths — the market day, the Ash crew, the toll-collector who walked, the Steward-claimant who tried Pressure and lost the room — and you have heard it, some of you, as a lesson in how to argue. A room with five parties and one Motion: *who takes the wreck in the sixth hab's channel?* The Ash crew wanted the body; the dead are the Ash's by licence, and the Ash believe that a body left unharvested is a soul wasted. The toll-collector wanted forty S for the channel. The Registrar's shelf had a line — Quell found it, Mesk read it out, one rung, no roll — showing that Hab Six had claimed the channel's salvage at the last Rereading, which voided the toll. And Corvane, who was not yet Steward and very much wanted to look like one, tried to seize the body *for the nursery's feed*, and pushed too hard, and the room turned from Hungry toward Hostile under him like a current changing direction.

What I have not told before is the part that happened *after* the Convocation, when the Motion was settled and the Ash took the body and the room cleared.

The Ash crew's Reader was an Amoeban called Varrow — ninety Beats a Reader, soft-bodied and patient and terrifyingly kind, the way the Ash often are. She had Read more dead than anyone in Sump Nine had met living. She laid the scout on the Ash shelf, and opened her own body around the dead one's head in that way Readers do, and stayed so for a long time. Then she withdrew, and her colour had changed.

"I can't render her," Varrow said.

The Ash crew went still. Corvane, at the door, went very still indeed.

"She has an Oath box unspent," Varrow said. "One. Sworn and not yet kept." She turned to Oro — not to the Registrar, not to the room; to *Oro* — and said, "We do not harvest a body whose Oaths are unspent. That debt is sacred. We will keep her until it is paid, or until the Rereading, whichever comes first." Her voice was very gentle. "Do you know what she swore, hauler?"

Oro did not. He had the strand under his fin, warm as a living thing, and he did not take it out.

"I can tell you the shape of it," Varrow said. "Not the words. Only what it smelled of." She hesitated, which a Reader almost never does. "It smelled of *listing*. Of a name in a register. Of — *seeing a child written down.*"

The room was empty but for the four of them: the Reader, the dead scout, the hauler, and Mesk, who had stayed because nobody had told them to go, and who was watching Oro the way the Immunans watch everything — closely, without judgement, missing nothing.

Afterward, outside in the noise of the Fair, Mesk said: "You didn't show her the strand."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I don't know what's in it."

"That's not why," Mesk said. They could not help it; it was the truth, and their body put it in their mouth. "You're afraid of what's in it."

Oro stopped moving.

It lasted perhaps a breath. For a Flagellan, a breath of stillness is a long time; it is the beginning of the ache. Mesk had never seen a Flagellan stop. They told me it was like watching a current decide something.

"Yes," Oro said.

"I can't lie to you," Mesk said. "So I won't tell you it's going to be all right. I don't know that. But I can read crystal. And I can keep a secret, as long as nobody asks me directly." A pause; a faint, cold, amused flicker in the marking-glands. "Try not to let anyone ask me directly."

That was the start of it. I want you to mark that, because this is a story about love as much as it is about water, and love in the Expanse does not begin with a look. It begins with an exchange of risks.

---

On the third Beat of the Fair, Oro gave Mesk a plasmid.

You know what a plasmid is. A small sequence, a knack, a little piece of what you know how to do, passed from body to body at a Hearth. Not a skill — something smaller and stranger: *how to calm a frightened bud*, *how to digest lipid without cramping*, *how to tell when a Speaker is shading the air*. Giving one is the deepest courtesy there is. Refusing one, offered at a Hearth, is an insult your budline will remember for a Tide.

The Seven Ladders had a plasmid that every one of them carried: a lullaby, of sorts. A pattern of pressure and scent that a hauler could breathe over a terrified body to slow its chemistry down. Sefa had given it to Oro when he was very small, and he had used it on every bud in the nursery, and on Dunmore once when the Barophil was crushed under a fallen cyst and screaming in ammonia.

He gave it to Mesk at the haulers' Hearth, at the shared meal, in front of everyone. It takes a Fugue Interval. You sit very close, and you breathe, and the other body learns.

Sefa watched from across the Hearth with her slow grey lashes and said nothing at all. But she smelled — Oro told me — of *worry*, and of something much older than worry, which he did not recognise until the Rendering.

"Why give me that one?" Mesk asked afterwards.

"Because you'll need it," Oro said. "You work for Quell. One day they'll come for her again. You'll need to calm somebody down."

"That isn't why either," said Mesk, who could not stop telling the truth even when it was cruel and especially when it was kind.

"No," Oro agreed. "It isn't."

At the end of the Fair the air was read for the Steward's office, and it did not reach Quorum, because the Pump breathed against Corvane as hard as a hundred and forty tired bodies could breathe — which is not as hard as one rich one can pay for, but is harder than you'd think. The office stayed empty. The store stayed locked. The Tally-Keeper, a frightened Epithalian called Henn, went on being three Beats behind on the colony's loan from the Brine Office, and went on being frightened.

And a Hegemony factor called Lune, from the Brine Office itself, took a room in the Dry pocket next to Corvane's, and stayed.

## III · UPHILL
### *Isotonic Rise, into the Drift Season*

> *Downhill is free. Uphill is a body. The whole economy is the argument about whose.*
> — the haulers' Hearth-saying

The strand would not open.

Mesk could read crystal — register crystal, archive crystal, the long protein libraries that the Nucleolus keeps — but a memory strand is not an archive. It is a piece of a self, and it opens only to the self it was made for, or to a Reader who can persuade it, and the scout had sealed hers to *the one I carried*. Oro could feel it warming to him when he held it. He could not make it speak.

"It needs a Lysosan," Mesk said. "Not a Reader. A *Lysosan*. She was one; it was made in a Lysosan body. It'll want a body that smells like home."

"Quell," said Oro.

"Quell won't do it." Mesk's glands flickered. "I asked. She said — " and here Mesk's voice did the thing it did when it was carrying someone else's words exactly, because Immunans cannot paraphrase without feeling they've lied — "*'I will not open another organism's last memory without the Ash in the room. That is how you become the kind of Registrar who erases things for money.'*"

"Then what?"

"The Tallow Market," Mesk said. "There's a Lysosan there who opens anything. Varrow says she's honest. Varrow says *honest* the way other people say *dangerous*."

The Tallow Market is three Beats upstream and a world away: a floating bazaar on a raft of congealed fat on the edge of the Lipid Plateaus, four hundred organisms, no weapons, and — this is the point of it — *no registers*. Nothing in the Tallow Market is read. Nobody is listed. You can buy a plasmid there that was stolen from a dying elder, or a Gene-Debt that someone cannot pay, or another organism's *Oath* — the obligation itself, transferred to you, binding you as if you had sworn it. It is the only place in the Expanse where a line in a Ledger can change hands without a Rite.

A hauler cannot leave his shift for six Beats. A registrar's apprentice cannot leave the shelves. So they did what every pair of young organisms with a secret has done since the Making: they lied about where they were going. Or rather Oro lied, and Mesk *didn't say*, which for an Immunan is a kind of heroism. Oro told the Pump he was hauling a Guild cargo uphill to the Plateaus for double wages. Mesk told Quell they were going to the Tallow Market to fetch a Lysosan who could open a strand, and Quell — who knew exactly what was happening, because the Lysosan lineage are information brokers to the bone — said only, "Take a cleaner. The Plateaus are full of parasites," and paid for a **Cleaner Pact** out of her own pouch, five S a Beat to a little Amoeban grooming-guild, so that the two of them would come back with nothing living in their membranes that hadn't been invited.

I tell you that so you know what Quell was. She was Unlisted. She had no rights. She paid for strangers' cleaning anyway.

---

They went upstream by the Currentpost lines — the Flow Guilds' charted currents, which carry you a region a Beat if you pay the toll, and which Oro could ride in his sleep. Mesk could not. Immunans are built for gates and ranks and registers, not for open water; they swim like a careful sentence. On the first night out, in the dim, in a dead calm between two currents, Mesk had to take a Dormant Cycle — six hours of real sleep, the long rest that every body needs — and there was no nest, no cyst, nothing secured, just the open water of the Mid-Strata and whatever drifted in it.

So Oro watched them sleep.

The haulers call it **incubation**: one body keeping watch while another takes its Dormant Cycle in the open. It is a mutualism — a small, priced, chemical favour, the kind that lets a body survive among other bodies. Every incubation writes a line: *I watched over you.* It is a little debt. It is also one of the most intimate things one organism can do for another in this world, because a body in its Dormant Cycle cannot mask itself. It smells of exactly what it is.

Oro moved all night — he had to — circling, a slow wake around the sleeping Immunan. And he learned what Mesk smelled of when Mesk could not help it. He told me it was *salt and clean count and something underneath that was lonely in a way he recognised*. He told me he decided then. He told me he decided before he knew there was anything to decide.

When Mesk woke, the first thing they said, before they were fully back in their body, was: "You stayed."

"I can't stop moving," Oro said. "Staying is easy. It's all the same water."

"That's a lie," said Mesk sleepily. "I can taste it."

"Yes," said Oro. "It is."

---

The Tallow Market at the Isotonic Rise is a sight I wish every one of you could have. Picture a raft of pale fat the size of a hab-hill, floating on a sea of slow sweet oil, strung with cables and lamp-moths and stalls, and every stall breathing a different temptation into the thick air. Barophil herders selling calves. Deep Choir pilgrims selling *silence* — an hour in their presence, during which your Stress will not rise. A Hegemony auditor in a sealed suit, buying debts. An old Flagellan oath-broker with a hundred crystals on strings around her body, every one of them an Oath that somebody had sworn and somebody else now owned.

"That one," the oath-broker told Mesk, tapping a dull crystal with her lash, "is a mother's promise to bring her bud home from the Wastes. The mother died. The bud's cousin sold it to me to pay for her Rendering. Now *I* am bound to bring the bud home. I'll sell it to you for thirty S. Then you'll be bound." She laughed. "Most of what I sell is somebody's grief. The Ash call it a theft. The Ash call everything a theft. You want the Lysosan? Under the third cable. She opens anything. She'll want something you didn't know you had."

The Lysosan under the third cable was very old and very small and almost entirely *other*: her body was crowded with sequences that were not hers, sitting in her like passengers on a skiff, and she spoke in a voice that kept changing as different ones took the tiller. She held the strand against her and tasted it a long while.

"Oh," she said finally, in a voice that was no one's in particular. "Oh, this is a brooder's strand."

"A what?" said Oro.

"A brooder. Somebody who carried something *inside* across water it couldn't cross alone. A child, usually. A sequence. A seed." The voices inside her shifted. "This one carried a child from the Wastes to the Mid-Strata. She swore an Oath to see it listed. The strand is for the child." She looked at Oro, and every passenger in her looked with her. "I can open it. But a brooder's strand opens *once*, and then it is spent, and a memory spent is gone from the world. It would be better opened at a Hearth. With the Ash in the room. Where the dead can be honoured as they are heard." A sudden sharp voice, nothing like the others: "*Whoever told you to bring it to the Tallow Market wanted it heard where nothing is registered. Ask yourselves why.*"

"Nobody told us," Mesk said. "We came because Quell wouldn't — "

They stopped. Mesk had heard it too.

Quell had not sent them. Quell had refused. It was Varrow who had said *the Tallow Market*. It was Varrow who had mentioned the Lysosan. And Varrow had said *honest* the way other people say *dangerous*.

"What do you want for it?" Oro asked. "For not opening it."

The old Lysosan smiled, and every passenger smiled differently.

"A line," she said. "One line in your Ledger. *Owed to the old one under the third cable: one true thing, when she asks.* I collect true things. They're worth more than Substrate on a raft with no registers." She held out the strand. "Take your memory home, children. Open it where it can be witnessed. Whatever is in it, somebody upstream wants it *un*-witnessed. That's all I know. That, and that the Brine Office paid me a visit two Beats ago, asking if anyone had come to me with a scout's strand."

They came home by the Lipidpost — the Ash's corpse-wagons, which go one region a Tide and stop at every Rendering ground and cannot be stopped or searched or censored, because censoring a funeral breaks the Sacred Code's second Precept and every Ash crew in the Expanse would come to the funeral's defence. It was slow. It was safe. They rode on the roof of a wagon full of the honoured dead, and the dead did not mind, and Mesk sat very close to Oro on the roof in the drift, and neither of them said anything about the Ledger line they now owed jointly to an old woman on a raft.

They got back to Sump Nine two Beats into the Drift Season. Oro owed the Pump six Beats' wages for the shift he'd missed, and the Brine Office five S for every one of those Beats, and an old Lysosan one true thing. His Ledger had three lines in the *You owe* column now. A Swollen organism may carry three. He was full.

And Sefa, at the haulers' Hearth, had begun to cough salt.

---

## IV · THE SALT VIGIL
### *Hypertonic Zenith*

> *Salt calls salt. Water finds the floor.*
> — proverb of the Dry, and of the Swollen, meaning opposite things

Let me tell you what the Hypertonic Zenith does to a colony, for those of you who have only ever lived in the soft Mid-Strata seasons.

Once a Tide, for a Drift Season, the whole world turns salty. It is the Osmotic Tide at its peak: every region of the Expanse goes hypertonic, and water — which always flows toward salt, always, it is the oldest law there is — begins to leave every body that does not hold enough salt to keep it. The dense survive. The Dry, in their sealed pockets, with their stored minerals and their concentrated interiors, barely notice; they close their doors and drink their salt-tea and are, if anything, a little more comfortable than usual. The Swollen *shrink*. The water goes out of them like breath. They move slower. They heal half as fast. Every Drift costs them more. The old ones, whose membranes have thinned with age, can lose more water in a Beat than they can drink.

At the Zenith, the colony holds the **Salt Vigil**: a Beat and a dim of fasting and rationing, when the water rights of every household are read aloud from the Vacuole's crystal and every organism learns, publicly, how much water it is owed. The Deep Choir sings its long note — one pressure-song, sustained from the beginning of the Beat to its end, by Barophil throats that can hold a note longer than you can hold a grudge. Arrears are audited. Thefts are tried the same night. And in the open sump, where everyone can watch, organisms with a quarrel that cannot wait settle it by **duel**.

That Zenith, Sump Nine's water rights were read aloud, and they were *short*.

Henn the Tally-Keeper stood at the Vacuole with the crystal and read the households one by one, and with every line her sheets tightened, because she could taste what everyone could taste: the store held less than the tally said. Not a little less. A *ladder's* worth less. Somewhere between the channel mouth and the Vacuole's cysts, a share of Sump Nine's water had gone, Beat after Beat, without anyone hauling it.

"Rationing at three-quarters," Henn said. Her voice was steady and her body was not. "Until the Fall. By order of the Tally."

The Swollen took it the way the Swollen always take it: quietly, and then not quietly. The haulers' Hearth that night was full of the smell of anger, which in the Expanse smells like hot iron and old blood. Dunmore sat in the middle of it like a boulder in a current. "Somebody," he said slowly, "is drinking our water."

"Corvane," said half the Hearth.

"Proof," said Dunmore.

There was no proof. There was a smell — the Dry pockets on the high wall were full and fat in a season when they should have been merely comfortable — but a smell is a rumour, and a rumour is not a line. *Fresh*, a rumour like that is. It lives a Beat. Anybody can disprove it. Corvane disproved it the next morning by opening his pocket's door to the Muster and letting him count the cysts, and the count was exactly what Corvane's household was owed, to the drop.

"He's moved it," said Oro.

"Then find where," said Mesk.

---

Here is the duel. Every Salt Vigil tale has a duel in it. This one is true.

The Pump, by the old custom of the Vigil, challenged the absent Steward's office for the water. Not Corvane — Corvane did not hold the office; he only wanted it. The challenge was to the *office*: the claim that the Vacuole's store, by the charter, belonged to the households and not to the treasury, and that the shortage should come out of the Dry pockets first. A Motion, sworn as an Oath by both sides in the open sump, the walls registered by the Porter so that what happened between them would be law.

Each side names a champion. The Pump named Grue.

Grue was the Muster — Barophil, vast, slow, a thing more like a hab-hill than an organism, with skin like pressed slate and a voice that could hold the long note for a Beat and a half. He was the colony's militia-captain. He did not need to fight for the Pump; it was not his office. He fought because he had been a hauler for six Great Cycles before he was the Muster, and because Sefa had fed him in the nursery when he was a bud.

The office's side named a champion too, because Corvane paid for one. She was a Kinetic Striker from upstream, a Flagellan mercenary with a wake like a blade, who arrived twice — once where you saw her, and once where she actually was. You know the kind. You've heard the rumour at the Rendering tables: *a Kinetic Striker killed an Oath-duel opponent by arriving twice.* It was her. Or it was her sister. They are always somebody's sister.

The whole of Sump Nine came to watch — watching an Oath-duel is a legal act, interrupting it is a scandal, and killing either duellist outside the duel's rules is a crime. They watched from the ladders, from the stalls, from the gel moat where the buds were pressed against the nursery membrane like a cluster of eggs. I was one of them. I was not born yet; I was a thing inside a thing inside a thing. But I have the memory of it, and I will tell you how it went.

The Striker hit Grue four times in the first Cycle. Kinetic blows, each one pushing him a Drift backward across the sump, each one cracking a little more of the slate of him. Grue did not move. Barophils do not move first; they are slow on the Frequency, it is their Frailty, they are always a heartbeat behind a fast room. He stood in the centre of the open sump and let the Striker break against him like water against a wall, and he sang.

He sang the long note. Under it — through it — the Striker came round for the fifth time, the killing time, arriving twice, and Grue *let her*. He let her first arrival hit him in the chest, and then, because he had been listening to the water all along, he put his great slow hand exactly where her second arrival was going to be.

He caught her. Like catching a fish.

And she yielded.

You know the Yield. A chemical white flag, flown with the whole body; an organism that spends a whole Cycle signalling surrender, in a scent it cannot fake. And whoever kills a yielding organism — in war, in a duel, anywhere — marks every witness with Stress and drops the Cohesion of everyone who saw it. Killing a duellist in front of a whole colony is the most expensive thing you can do in the Expanse.

Grue held the Striker for a long breath with every organism in Sump Nine watching. Then he opened his hand.

The Motion passed. The office conceded: the shortage would come out of the Dry pockets first. It was the right result, and the whole sump knew it, and it changed exactly nothing, because there was no Steward to enforce it and no proof of where the water was going, and the Dry pockets went on being full.

That is the thing about a duel. A duel settles in one scene what a war would settle in a Tide and a hundred dead. It does not settle whether the winner can collect.

---

Sefa died at the dim.

I am sorry to come at it so plainly. There is no other way to come at it. She was old, and she was Swollen, and the Zenith had been taking water out of her for half a Drift Season, and she had given half her ration to the nursery because the charter said every hab feeds one nursery shift and she was too old to haul and nursery feed was what she had left to give. She had been coughing salt since the Rise. She coughed salt through the duel. She was sitting at the haulers' Hearth listening to Grue's long note fade when her lashes stopped moving.

Oro was hauling. Of course he was. It was the dim; the night pumps run in the dim, and a Flagellan has to move. Mesk came for him. Mesk swam the third ladder top to bottom, which an Immunan should not be able to do, to find him on the tow-line, and said, in the plain way they could not help: "Sefa is dead. She died at the Hearth. She wasn't alone. Dunmore was with her. She didn't hurt."

Oro stopped moving.

He stopped for a long time. Long enough for the ache to come. Long enough for the grey nothing that comes after the ache — the thing he had always been afraid of — to start creeping in at the edges of his lashes. Long enough that Mesk, who did not know what else to do, sat beside him on the tow-line in the dim and breathed over him the only thing they had ever been given that was meant for this.

The Seven Ladders' lullaby. The plasmid he had given them at the Fair.

It worked. Plasmids are not magic; they are chemistry, and chemistry does not care whether you deserve it. Oro's body slowed, and steadied, and after a while it began, very slightly, to move again. He told me the first thing he felt was not grief. It was *being owed*. Sefa had fed him every Beat of his life and he had never once paid her back.

"She signed a Digestion Pact," Mesk said quietly, when he could hear it. "Last Rendering. Varrow told me."

A Digestion Pact. Do you know what that is, those of you from the soft regions? It is a mutualism, like incubation, like grooming — one of the twelve acts organisms do for each other. You sign your body over to the Ash *while you are still alive*. They pay you an advance, half of what your harvest will be worth, in Substrate, today. And when you die, your body is theirs to read and render, and they will enforce your unspent Oaths whether you like it or not.

Sefa had sold her own body to the Ash a Tide ago. She had sold it for fifty S.

And she had paid fifty S into the Seven Ladders' debt, against the principal, for the first time in two Great Cycles.

"She was paying it down," Oro said. "She was paying *my* share down."

"Yes," said Mesk.

"I didn't know."

"She asked Varrow not to tell you," Mesk said. "Varrow told me because I asked her directly." A flicker of cold Immunan humour, very small, the size of a grief. "People should stop asking me things directly."

---

## V · THE RENDERING
### *Isotonic Fall*

> *The Ash read the dead because somebody must. A body that is not read is a story that is not told, and a story that is not told is a soul thrown in the water.*
> — the Reclaimers' Ash, as they say it at every Rendering

Two Beats of the Rendering, every Isotonic Fall. You are sitting in the second of them now. You know how it goes: the year's dead brought to the Ash Rows and hung in their husks, and the Readers going among them one by one, opening their own bodies around the heads of the dead and reading the last of what they knew. A body's last hours stay in it a long time, if nobody renders it first. The Ash think a soul is mostly memory. They might be right.

The Reading of an organism is done at its own Hearth, in front of its kin. That is the law. So on the first Beat of that Rendering, Varrow came to the haulers' Hearth with her crew and Sefa's body in its husk, and every hauler in Sump Nine sat around the warm stone, and Mesk sat at Oro's side, and the scout from the channel hung in her own husk on the Ash shelf behind them all, still unrendered, still owed.

Varrow read Sefa for a long time.

When she came out of it, she did not speak to the room. She spoke to Oro. That was the second time she had done that, and both times it meant the same thing: *this is yours, and I am sorry*.

"She has a confession," Varrow said, "and she wants it heard by her ward."

The Hearth went cold.

"She wants you to know," Varrow said, very gently, in the voice the Ash use to carry the words of the dead exactly, "that you are not of her budline. That you were *brought*. That twenty Drift Seasons ago, at a Rendering very like this one, a Lysosan scout came down the Fork of the Second Flow from the Wastes with a bud *inside her* — brooded, the way the Ash carry seeds, the way a mother carries what she cannot leave behind. She had carried it across the Wastes because the colony it came from had been struck from every register. Unlisted. The whole of it. A purge. And an Unlisted bud has no name, no line, no Hearth, no water, no right to exist inside any membrane in the Expanse."

Oro did not move. He told me later he had known since the channel. He told me that knowing a thing and hearing it said at your Hearth are not the same thing at all.

"The scout gave the bud to Sefa," Varrow said, "and Sefa took it as her ward. A **Wardship**. A Rite at a Hearth; a line that cannot be transferred. And then Sefa went to the Registrar of that day and asked her to write the bud into the birth register as a Seven Ladders. Flagellan. Born in Sump Nine. A forged line. The Registrar did it, for a debt." Varrow's voice did not change. "The Registrar was Quell."

Every hauler at that Hearth turned, slowly, to look at the door, where Quell was standing — Quell the Unlisted Registrar, who had come to every Reading at every Hearth in Sump Nine for six Tides, because nobody had ever told her she couldn't. She did not leave. She did not look away. She stood in the doorway and let the whole Pump smell what she was.

"*I owe her for it*," Varrow said — Sefa's words, in Varrow's mouth. "*I owe Quell a debt I will not live to pay. I owe the Brine Office a debt my budline will not live to pay. I owe the child the truth, and that one I am paying now. Oro. You were never mine. You were always mine. Both. I am sorry I made you a lie to keep you alive. I would do it again.*"

That was the end of the Reading.

Sefa's body went to the Rendering ground in the morning, and was rendered properly, the Ash's way, and every part of her that could feed something fed something, and her last memory went into a strand that Varrow gave to Oro, and that is the strand I carry. I have it under my fin now. I will not play it for you. Some memories are Hearth memories, and this is not my Hearth.

---

That night — the dim between the two Beats of the Rendering, five Tides ago — Oro went to the Ash shelf where the scout still hung, and Mesk went with him, and Quell, and Varrow. And Dunmore, because Dunmore had been the one sitting with Sefa, and a Barophil who has started a thing finishes it.

And Oro opened the strand.

He did not need the old Lysosan from the Tallow Market. He understood that now. The strand had been made in a body that smelled like home, and he *was* home; he was the thing that had been brooded. It opened to him the moment he wanted it to. A brooder's strand opens once. He knew that, too. He opened it anyway, in front of witnesses, at a Hearth, with the Ash in the room. Where it could be heard properly.

Her name was Yarrow. Yarrow-who-went-ahead.

I will not give you all of it. There is a great deal in a life. But I will give you the three things that matter to this tale, because they are the hooks the rest of it hangs on, and because Yarrow wanted them told.

*The first:* she was not his mother. His mother had stayed behind in the struck colony in the Wastes, because she was too sick to cross, and had divided one last time, and put the bud in Yarrow's hands, and said *take this somewhere it can be written down*. Yarrow had sworn the Oath on the spot — *to see the child listed* — and it was the one Oath box she had never spent, because she had never known whether the forged line counted. She had come back to Sump Nine twenty Drift Seasons later, sick with a sequence that was winning its argument with her, to find out. She had died three Beats short of the Hearth.

*The second:* she had come back by the Fork of the Second Flow, and on the way down, two hab-lengths above Sump Nine's somatic gate, she had found a **channel**.

You know what a channel is. An illegal pore. Membranes are meant to read everything that crosses them — that is the first Precept of the Sacred Code, *nothing crosses a colony's membrane unread* — and channel-makers are the smugglers who open little holes so that goods can leak through without being read. Yarrow was a Pathogen Scout; she could smell a pore the way a Warden smells a forged token. And this pore did not let goods *in*. It let water *out*. A thin, constant, patient bleed, from the cysts at the channel mouth — the cysts the haulers hauled — up through the old protein lattice under the north wall of the gel moat, the lattice whose supports had been so neatly cut, and into a sealed pipe that ran, she followed it, all the way up the inside of the north wall.

To the Dry pocket on the highest ledge. Corvane's pocket. And the pocket next to it, where a Hegemony factor called Lune had taken a room.

*The third thing* was the part of the strand Yarrow had recorded last, in the channel, three Beats from death, when she knew she was not going to reach the Hearth. She had laid it into the strand so carefully that every one of them in that room smelled it at once, as if she were standing among them.

*Two of them in the pipe-room. The Dry one and the Hegemony one. The Dry one says: the tally fails by the Swell. The Brine Office calls the loan. The colony defaults. The Hegemony buys the charter. And I am Steward under the Hegemony's charter, and the water is mine to sell. The Hegemony one says: and if anyone smells the pipe? The Dry one says: who? The Swollen? They'll be too thirsty to smell anything.*

The strand went dark. It was spent. Yarrow was gone from the world.

The Ash shelf was very quiet.

"That's a line," said Dunmore at last. "That's not a rumour. That's a *witnessed line*."

"It's a dead woman's memory," said Quell. "In a strand that no longer exists. Witnessed by an Unlisted Registrar, an Ash Reader, a hauler with a forged birth, a Barophil, and an apprentice who can't lie." She smiled without any humour at all. "It's the best evidence I've ever heard, and Corvane will have every one of us struck off before we can say it at a Convocation."

"Then we say it at a Quorum," said Oro.

"Then they'll ask how you knew where the pipe was," said Quell. "And the honest answer starts with *a scout brought me here as a seed*. And the moment that's said in a room with a register in it, you are Unlisted, Oro. You have no birth. You have no line. You will have no water, no Hearth, no Ledger. Corvane will make sure of it. *That's* why Varrow sent you to the Tallow Market. To open the strand where nothing is registered. To give you the proof without anyone ever learning where you came from."

Varrow said nothing. Varrow had said everything she meant to, at the Fair.

Oro looked at Mesk.

And Mesk — Mesk, who could not lie, who would answer truthfully any question anyone put to them directly — Mesk looked back at him, and in front of all of them said: "I want to conjugate with you. At the Contract Fair. Next Rise. I want it in front of everyone."

The Hearth went perfectly still.

"Mesk," said Quell, very softly, "if you link your membrane with his, and he is Unlisted — "

"I know," said Mesk. "I'm not asking whether I *can*. Immunans don't ask whether. I'm telling you what's true." They turned back to Oro. "That's what's true."

Oro did not answer, then. You must forgive him. He was a hauler whose whole life had just been read aloud by the dead, and he had been asked the one question that has no safe answer, by the one organism in the Expanse who would not let him pretend.

He went back out to the night pumps. Of course he did. A Flagellan has to move.

But he took Mesk with him. And all that dim they hauled water up the third ladder together, cyst after cyst — an Immunan hauling, which Sump Nine had never seen — and at the top of every landing, he stopped, and Vented, and did not move on until Mesk had caught up.

Dunmore saw it from the fourth landing. Dunmore told me. "Vent at the top," Dunmore said. "Not halfway. Halfway, your body learns that halfway is where you stop." And then, after a long Barophil pause: "*He was stopping at every landing. For the other one. Never seen a Flagellan learn anything so fast.*"

---

## VI · THE SWELL
### *Hypotonic Zenith*

> *Masks are lawful. Nobody is lying. Everybody is lying. Welcome to the Swell.*
> — a carnival barker at Knot Nine, every Hypotonic Zenith

And then the Tide turned, as Tides do, and the water came back.

At the Hypotonic Zenith the whole Expanse goes soft and wet and dilute, and water — which always flows toward salt — floods *into* every body that holds more salt than the world around it. The Swollen, who have spent half a Tide shrinking, fill out like buds after a meal. They move faster. They heal. Every Drift costs them less. And the Dry, in their sealed and salted pockets, begin to *bloat*: water forcing its way into them through every membrane, their pumps straining day and night to bail it out. For one Drift Season a Tide, the poor are strong and the rich are uncomfortable, and every colony in the Expanse knows what happens next.

The Swell. A Pulse Ten of carnival. The Swollen rule the streets. Masks are lawful — the fourth Precept of the Code, *tonicity is truth*, is suspended by custom, so that for ten Beats anyone may smell like anything — and every Issue the Pump backs starts the air a little thicker in its favour. There are current-races in the channel. There are mock-elders crowned by the haulers and paraded up the ladders on cyst-sledges. There are riots, sometimes. There are always riots, somewhere.

That Swell, Sump Nine had a strike.

The haulers stopped on the first Beat. Dunmore called it, at the Hearth, in his slow way: "We carry water uphill to the Dry. The Dry are drinking our water through a pipe. We will not carry another cyst up the ladders until the colony knows it." Nobody in Sump Nine could prove the pipe. Everybody in Sump Nine had heard of it, by then — the rumour had gone from Fresh to Repeated in a Drift Season, the way true rumours do — and a Repeated rumour is hard to disprove, and Corvane did not try. He simply stopped coming down from his pocket.

Without the Pump, the Vacuole emptied. Every price in the colony crept toward scarce. The rationing that had ended at the Fall came back. The colony's Interior — its unrest, its Stress — climbed to five and stayed there: a strike. The Assembly sat for two Beats and could not find eight voices for anything, because the Steward's chair was empty and the Printwright's chair was empty and the Registrar's chair belonged to someone the register said did not exist.

And Henn, the Tally-Keeper, came to the haulers' Hearth on the fourth Beat of the Swell with her sheets wound so tight she could hardly speak, and said: "The tally will fail by the end of the Swell. I'm eleven Beats behind on the Brine Office loan. Factor Lune called it this morning. If Sump Nine cannot pay, Sump Nine defaults, and the Hegemony buys our charter." She looked around the Hearth. "Lune has already filed the Issue. *Sell the charter to the Brine Office, to clear the colony's debt.* It goes on the air tonight. It will be read at the end of the Swell."

"And who's Steward under the Hegemony's charter?" asked Dunmore, who already knew.

Henn did not answer. Henn did not need to.

---

I will not walk you through every round of that Quorum. You know how a Quorum goes. Every organism who cares breathes signal into the Hearth air, Beat after Beat, until the air is thick enough with one answer that the colony *knows*. Speak at the Hearth; move the crowd; swear it; court an organ; call a faction; pay for signal. The Pump spoke and moved and swore, and a hundred and forty Swollen bodies at the height of their Tide-strength breathed against the sale as hard as they could breathe. Corvane, bloating in his pocket, *paid*. Cyst after cyst of rendered signal, poured into the Hearth air by servants in masks.

And still the air did not move the way it should have. Night after night, Wend the Speaker tasted it and said, "*Sell* gains." The Pump was breathing its lungs out, and the air was barely changing.

"He's quenching," said Quell, on the sixth Beat.

You know what quenching is. It is the gravest political crime in the Expanse. Not lying in the air: *making the air lie*. An enzyme, breathed into the Hearth, that destroys the other side's signal before it can accumulate. A quench leaves a residue, and anyone can hunt for it — but only *after* the Issue is read, and by then the colony already *knows*, and a colony that has reached Quorum on a stolen question still acts on it.

"Can you prove it?" said Oro.

"After," said Quell. "Only after. And after, the charter is sold, and the Hegemony's Wardens are at the gate, and I'm the first organism they'll strike."

"Then we need to Reveal the pipe *before*," said Mesk. "In front of the colony. Not a rumour. A *line*."

"The strand is spent," said Varrow.

"Then we show them the pipe itself," said Oro.

---

Now. This is the part bards fight over. Every Hearth in the Mid-Strata tells it differently. I am going to tell it the way I remember it, because I *do* remember it, and I will tell you how in a moment.

The Swell's great sport is the current-race: organisms riding the channel current through a marked course, three checkpoints, Momentum allowed, bets running to thousands of S. Sump Nine's course ran from the somatic gate, round the gel moat, under the north wall — under the old cut lattice — and back to the gate. Every hauler in the colony knew it. Every Dry pocket on the north wall overlooked it. And on the last Beat of the Swell, with the whole colony masked and watching and the Quorum to be read at the twentieth Interval, Oro-of-the-Seven-Ladders entered the race.

He was a Flagellan at the height of a Hypotonic Zenith. He was faster than anyone in the water. He led from the gate. He led round the moat. And at the second checkpoint, under the north wall, where the pipe went into the lattice, he did not take the turn.

He went *into* the lattice.

The haulers on the ladders saw him go. The masked crowd on the moat saw him go. And then — this is the part every Hearth agrees on — the channel current, which had been flowing past the lattice for twenty Drift Seasons, found a hole where a hauler had torn it open, and went in after him, and the pipe that Corvane's channel-maker had hidden inside the north wall *burst*.

Water came out of the wall. A great pale fan of it, bright with the colony's own scent, pouring backward out of the dark where it had been bleeding upward for a Tide. Every organism in Sump Nine smelled it at once: *our water. Our water, coming out of the wall under the Dry pockets.* You could not fake that. You could not mask it. Precept 4 was suspended, and it did not matter, because a pipe is not an organism and does not get to wear a mask.

And the lattice under the north wall of the gel moat — the lattice whose protein supports had been cut, Tides ago, by someone who wanted the pipe to be easy to reach — began to fall.

Onto the nursery.

Forty buds of every lineage, pressed against the nursery membrane to watch the race.

---

Oro told me what happened next. Tannet told me. Grue told me, in his slow way, a Tide later, holding the long note for a full breath between every sentence. I will tell you what they told me, and then I will tell you what I remember.

Oro could have come out of the wall. The race was there, the crowd was there, the burst pipe was there, Reveal enough for any Quorum in the Expanse. He could have swum back to the gate and stood at the Hearth and said *the colony knows*, and the colony would have known.

He did not come out of the wall. He went *under* the falling lattice, and braced it.

A Flagellan. A body that cannot stand still. A body that, if it does not move, finds the ache, and after the ache the grey nothing that he had been afraid of his whole life. He put his back against a falling wall of protein lattice above forty buds and he *held still*.

He held it for a Fugue Interval. You can ask the Muster how long that is for a Flagellan. The ache came, and he held. The grey came, and he held. Tannet got the first ten buds out through the moat's far side. The haulers came down the ladders and got another twenty. And when Oro's lashes had stopped moving and his body had gone dull and grey and he could no longer have said his own name, Grue the Muster arrived, slow as a mountain, singing the long note, and put his vast slate shoulders under the lattice beside the hauler's, and held it while the last ten buds came out.

They pulled Oro out of the wall unconscious. He did not move for a full Beat afterwards. Mesk sat with him. Mesk breathed the Seven Ladders' lullaby over him, the whole Beat, until his chemistry remembered how to argue with the water again.

That is the story every Hearth tells.

Here is what I remember. *I remember the grey.* I remember it from the inside. I remember thinking, in that grey, that it was not boring after all. It was very, very quiet, and somewhere at the bottom of it someone was singing a long note, and I was not afraid.

You understand now how I remember. Hold that. I am nearly there.

---

The air was read at the twentieth Interval, as it had to be.

The whole of Sump Nine crowded the main sump, masked and unmasked, the haulers wet from the moat and the nursery buds bundled in Tannet's sheets. Corvane came down from his pocket for the first time in the Swell, bloated and furious, and stood at the Hearth with Lune at his side, and before Wend could taste the air, Corvane said:

"A question. Before the reading. I have the right; I am a party to the Issue." He turned his great salted body toward the Registrar's shelf, where Quell stood — and beside her, Mesk. "The hauler who burst the wall. The colony is about to act on what he showed it. The colony should know who he *is*. Ask the Registrar's apprentice. The Immunan." A smile, Dry as a vault. "Immunans cannot lie."

The sump went silent.

"Apprentice," said Corvane. "Directly, then. Is Oro-of-the-Seven-Ladders truly listed in the register of Sump Nine?"

Mesk could have refused. An Immunan may refuse to answer if an Oath forbids it — and Mesk had not sworn one; they had wanted to, at the Fair, and the Fair had not come yet. They could have refused anyway, and paid for it: Stress, one fraying of the membrane for every sentence of silence, until their body gave out. Some Immunans have died that way, refusing. It is in the songs.

Mesk looked across the sump at the haulers' Hearth, where Oro lay unmoving in Dunmore's arms.

And Mesk said: "No."

I want you to smell that word. Breathe it in. That was the sound of a heart going through a register like a blade.

"No," Mesk said again, and their voice did not shake, because Immunans do not lie and do not tremble. "He was brought here as a brooded seed by a Lysosan scout named Yarrow-who-went-ahead, from a colony in the Wastes that was struck from every register. His birth line was forged. He is not listed. He is not of the Seven Ladders by blood. He is Sefa's ward by Rite. That is all true." And then, without any pause at all, in exactly the same flat, terrible, truthful voice: "*And the water in your pocket is ours, Corvane. The pipe ends in your pocket and in the Hegemony factor's. Yarrow-who-went-ahead followed it up the wall and recorded it in her strand, and I heard that strand opened at the Rendering, with an Ash Reader and a Barophil and the Registrar as witnesses. You quenched the air. Every Beat of the Swell. I can taste the residue on you from here.*"

Corvane opened his mouth.

And the most credible witness in the Expanse — an Immunan, a body that *cannot lie* — stood at the Registrar's shelf and smelled, to every organism in Sump Nine, of exactly what they were.

Wend the Speaker tasted the air. Wend, who was honest enough most of the time and not quite honest enough when it mattered, tasted the air and, for once in her life, found it mattered enough. She did not read the Issue. She hunted the quench. It took her a Fugue Interval. The residue was everywhere.

The Quorum was void. Colony Cohesion fell by two, because a colony whose air has been stolen is a colony that has been robbed of its own mind. The Lysosome assayed Corvane that night — Varrow reading his body in front of the Hearth, the trial run as a Convocation with the Pump and the Tally and the Registrar's shelf in the room — and the reading was true, and the colony reached Quorum on it before dawn, and knew.

Corvane of the Sealed Pocket was **Unlisted**. Struck from every register Sump Nine shared. The irony was not lost on anyone, least of all him.

Lune, the Hegemony factor, was never charged. The Hegemony never breaks a contract in writing; it only reinterprets one for a Tide. Lune left by the Bridge post the next morning with a stamped letter explaining that the Brine Office had been *deceived by a local agent*, and the loan was quietly given ten Beats' grace, and the charter was not sold, and Sump Nine was still Sump Nine.

And Oro-of-the-Seven-Ladders, who had saved forty buds and a colony's water, woke up on the second Beat after the Swell to find he did not exist.

## VII · TENANCY
### *Isotonic Rise, and after*

> *The register is the colony. To be unlisted is to be outside the world's legal body.*
> — the Warden Circles, as the Codex records them

An Unlisted organism has no water.

That is not cruelty. That is arithmetic. The water tally is assessed by hearth, and an Unlisted organism has no hearth; the Vacuole's crystal has no line to read its ration from. It may eat at a Hearth if the Hearth will have it. It may haul, if the Pump will pay it off the books. It may not sign, may not swear, may not conjugate, may not be protected. A Warden may detain it on sight. It is outside the world's legal body.

The Pump would have hidden him forever. Dunmore said so; Grue said so; every hauler with a bud who had been in the nursery that Swell said so. Quell said more than that. Quell came to him on the third Beat with her shelves under her arm and said: "I can write you a line. A new one. Honest this time — or as honest as a forged thing can be. *Oro, ward of Sefa, listed by the Registrar of Sump Nine.* It would hold until the Rereading. Maybe longer."

"Written by an Unlisted Registrar," said Oro.

"Yes."

"For a debt."

"For nothing," said Quell. "For forty buds."

He thought about it for a long time. He told me he was tempted. He told me he thought about Sefa, who had made him a lie to keep him alive and said, from inside her own death, *I would do it again*. He told me he thought about Corvane, who was built of lies all the way down, and who was now exactly what Oro was.

"No," he said. "I won't be built on a lie twice. Sefa paid enough for the first one."

So there was nowhere in Sump Nine for him to be.

---

And then, at the Isotonic Rise, Old Mother Brine came down the Fork of the Second Flow.

You know Old Mother Brine. Every Flagellan does. She is a **Host-City**: a Vast saline amoeboid, slow and motherly, carrying thirty thousand tenants in her body cavities and the Flow Guilds' great headquarters in her left vacuole, migrating with the Tide from the Great Flows to the Mid-Strata to the Rim and back again. She does not read registers. Her immune cells read *scent* — whether you have paid your rent, one ATP a Beat fed into her at the Rent Wells, whether you smell of her tolerance or not. A Host-City does not care if you are listed. A Host-City cares if you are a good tenant.

She anchored at Sump Nine's channel mouth for one Beat — uninvited, as Host-Cities always are — and every Swollen hauler in the colony went down to the gate to look at her, because she was the size of a hab-hill and warm as a Hearth and full of the noise of thirty thousand lives.

"She'll take you," said Grue. "She takes anyone who pays."

"I know," said Oro.

He went to the Contract Fair first. That was the Fair where he and Mesk should have conjugated, in front of everyone, at the Hearth. They could not. A conjugation needs a witness from each budline, and an Unlisted organism has no budline; and an Immunan who links their membrane with an Unlisted body becomes, in the eyes of every Warden ward in the Expanse, *not quite standard* themselves. Mesk would have done it. Mesk said so, flatly, at the Hearth, in front of the whole Pump: "I'll lose the register. I don't care."

And Oro, who had learned from Mesk what it costs to say a true thing out loud, said: "*I* care. Quell is stepping down. She told me. She wants you to have the shelves. A Registrar who can't lie. Do you know what that would mean for every bud in that nursery? Every one of them would be written down *true*. Nobody would ever have to forge a line again." His lashes moved, very slightly, all the time. "That's worth more than me."

"That's a lie," said Mesk.

"Yes," said Oro. "It is. Take the shelves anyway."

So instead of conjugating, they swore Oaths. That is allowed, even to the Unlisted, if no register is asked; an Oath is written in your own chromatin, and chromatin does not care about paperwork. It cost them nothing to swear, because it was the Fair.

Mesk swore: *I will write you down.*

Oro swore: *I will come back to be read.*

And they wrote a line in each other's Ledgers, in the column that says *Owed to you*, witnessed by Quell and Dunmore and Grue and an Ash Reader called Varrow:

*Owed to Oro: one true line, at the Rereading.*

*Owed to Mesk: one body, at the end, to read.*

Then Oro-of-the-Seven-Ladders paid one ATP into Old Mother Brine's Rent Well and swam into her, and she lifted her anchor, and was gone up the Fork of the Second Flow with the Rise.

---

You can guess the rest. You have been guessing it since I told you my name.

He was a courier aboard Old Mother Brine for five Tides. A Pheromological Messenger, in the end — the Guilds took him on, because a Flagellan who has held still under a falling wall can carry anything anywhere. He carried trails across every region of the Expanse. He carried them through Hypertonic storms and Lysosomal fogs and once, the story goes, through a Warden purge, with a whole colony's register hidden in his fins so that the Wardens could not strike what they could not find. He signed a Digestion Pact with the Ash at the first Rendering he could reach, and paid fifty S into the Seven Ladders' debt, against the principal. The debt was the Ladders'. He was a Ladder by Rite, if not by register. He paid it down every Tide after that. He never stopped owing it. He never stopped paying.

And three Tides ago, in the warm left vacuole of Old Mother Brine, he divided.

You know what that means. One body becomes two, and both remember being one. The new bud was born carrying every memory the old body had, up to the moment of division: the third ladder, the scout in the channel, the plasmid at the Fair, the incubation in the open water, the duel, the Rendering, the grey under the lattice, the long note at the bottom of it. Everything. And after the division, the two bodies went their separate ways, as divisions do, and made separate memories, and the bud — being Flagellan, being a drifter, being a body that could not stand still — became a messenger like the old one, and learned to tell trails at Hearths for money.

My name is Lisk-of-the-Seven-Ladders. I was Oro, until three Tides ago. Then I was me.

## THE HEARTH, AGAIN

There. You see now why the Registrar has not stopped me. They never could stop a true thing once it started.

Oro died seven Beats ago, in a Hypertonic squall in the Rim, carrying a trail he never finished. His body came here by the Lipidpost — the Ash's corpse-wagons, which go one region a Tide and stop at every Rendering ground and cannot be stopped or censored — because he had a Digestion Pact with the Ash, and a line in a Ledger that said *Owed to Mesk: one body, at the end, to read.* He is hanging in the Ash Rows behind you. Between Sefa's old husk-hook and Yarrow's.

Yes. Yarrow's. She is still here. The Ash kept her, as Varrow said they would: *until the Oath is paid, or until the Rereading, whichever comes first.*

And this season — this very Drift Season, friends, this one we are sitting in — is the **Rereading**. The Great Cycle's turning. Sixteen Tides, and the registers are purged, and old debts are re-read, and every line in every Ledger in the Expanse is read aloud as though it happened yesterday. And the Unlisted, at the Rereading, may apply to be listed again.

Registrar. It's your part now. Come and say it. I've carried it all this way.

---

*Here the bard falls silent, and the tellers at every Hearth since have told it the same way: the Immunan Registrar of Sump Nine stands up from behind the Muster, with the register crystal in their hands, and walks to the Ash shelf, and reads the line aloud. Immunans cannot lie, so nobody has ever doubted the words.*

*"Oro. Brought as a brooded seed from a struck colony in the Lysosomal Wastes by Yarrow-who-went-ahead. Ward of Sefa of the Seven Ladders, by Rite. Hauler of the Pump of Sump Nine. Held the north wall of the gel moat at the Swell, and forty buds came out from under it alive. Listed — at the Rereading, by the Registrar of Sump Nine — as a citizen of this colony, with all its rights, from the day he was carried in. The debt of the Seven Ladders to the Brine Office: paid in full, this Tide, from his Digestion Pact. Witnessed by the Registrar. Witnessed by the Pump. Witnessed by the Ash."*

*And Yarrow's last Oath box — "to see the child listed" — is spent, at last, and the Ash render her that night beside him.*

*And the Registrar of Sump Nine sits with the Reader while Oro is read, as the Ledger says they are owed, and nobody knows what the Reader finds in his last memory, because the Registrar asks her not to say, and the Reader is Ash, and keeps it.*

*But the haulers say — and the haulers are not Immunans, and may be lying — that the Registrar breathed a lullaby over him while he was read. The Seven Ladders' lullaby. A plasmid that calms a frightened body. Given at a Fair, a long time ago, to someone who would need it.*

---

*Every body is a debt. Some are paid in Substrate, some in labour, some in Oath.*

*The ones we sing about were paid in water.*

*— here ends the trail of Oro-of-the-Seven-Ladders, as told at the Rendering of Sump Nine in the year of the Rereading, and at every Hearth since that would have it.*
